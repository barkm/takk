5. **Evaluation harness** — done: `evaluation.py` (see README). Evaluates a similarity matrix: per query and sign, k references by different signers other than the query's; pooled ROC-AUC and EER over the positive and all negative trials; top-1/top-5 identification; per-sign metrics; 5 reference draws; 95% bootstrap CIs over signs. A full evaluation of the val split takes 20–35 s; for checks during training use one k, one draw and no bootstrap.

## Decisions

- **ASL Citizen is the primary dataset** instead of Kaggle ASL Signs: ~11x the vocabulary (the main lever for unseen signs), 52 signers, both hands free, and videos so the landmark extractor is under our control and matches what the system will use in practice. Kaggle ASL Signs is one-handed smartphone signing with landmarks from a removed MediaPipe version.

- **Landmark extractor:** MediaPipe Tasks `HolisticLandmarker` (successor of the legacy Holistic) in video mode with default settings, on the CPU with 8 worker processes (`extraction.py`). Its landmarks match the Kaggle layout and hand label convention. MediaPipe's GPU mode was rejected: the holistic model fails on the GPU, and the separate face/hand/pose models run on the GPU but scale worse than the CPU (see findings).
- **Frame rate:** each clip's source fps is stored in the store metadata (null when unknown), so sequences can be resampled to a common rate.
- **Resumable extraction:** long extractions write the store in chunks of 1,024 clips and resume from the last finished chunk (`write_store_resumable`).
- **Raw ASL Citizen data is kept** (zip and videos), e.g. for re-extraction or rendering video frames.
- **Splits** (`splits.py`): signs are assigned to train/val/test (~80/10/10) by a hash of the sign label, stable across datasets. Test = test signs by the official ASL Citizen test signers (held out completely). Train and val share the official train and val signers; val = val signs by those signers, so it measures unseen signs but not unseen signers. Chosen over the official signer split (train/val/test signers), which left only 31,909 training clips (57% of all clips unused) and 2–5 signers per val sign, too few for 5-shot validation.
- **One fixed split during development**, with bootstrap confidence intervals over test signs; retraining on several sign folds (each fold is a full training run) only for final numbers or comparisons too close to call.

- **Training data loader design** (step 4): PyTorch. Two stages: a deterministic preparation, cached in `data/prepared/`, and random augmentation at training time.
  - Landmarks: hands + upper body + face reference points (60) by default, lips as a later experiment; x and y only by default (z is noisy).
  - Image aspect ratio: store each clip's frame width and height, and convert coordinates to correct proportions before normalizing (ASL Citizen mixes 4:3 and 16:9).
  - Normalization per clip: center on the mean shoulder midpoint, scale by the mean shoulder width.
  - Mirroring so the dominant hand is always on the same side (the `right_hand` slot): a one-handed clip (both hands in < 20% of its frames with a hand) by its detected hand; a two-handed clip by the signer's handedness, the majority over their one-handed clips. At inference the user's handedness is therefore needed (e.g. an app setting, or estimated from their one-handed signs). Training without mirroring but with random mirroring augmentation is a later experiment.
  - Time: trim to the first to last frame with a hand plus ~0.1 s, resample to 30 fps, cap at 128 frames by resampling; variable length with padding and a mask.
  - Hand gaps: interpolate gaps of up to ~5 frames; longer gaps are zeros with a per-frame hand-present flag.
  - Exclude broken clips (no hands, hands for under ~0.2 s or over ~10 s), after checking examples.
  - Augmentation: rotation, scale, shift, shear, speed change, frame dropping, random hand dropout.
  - The loader outputs normalized coordinates and hand-present flags; derived features (velocities, hand-local coordinates) are computed in the model.

- **Next direction after the architecture experiments: more data, starting with Sem-Lex** (step 8), since architectures and regularization converge to the same level. Measuring generalization to unseen signers (with training signers held out, not the test split) can run alongside.

- **MM-WLAuslan is used as training data only** (Sem-Lex is blocked on access): all its signs are new classes, ASL Citizen's val and test stay unchanged, so results remain comparable with earlier runs and measure whether more signs, even of another language, help with unseen ASL signs. Without signer ids it cannot be evaluated k-shot with references by other signers; asking the authors for the ids would enable that later. Clips have a null `signer`.
- **MM-WLAuslan scope:** RGB of the front Kinect camera (closest to ASL Citizen's frontal webcams), subsets Train, Valid, Test-STU, Test-ITW and Test-SYN (64,300 clips, 20 per sign); not Test-TED (frames removed and speed changed, which can cut signs). The side cameras film the same performances from other angles, so they are left for later.
- **Sign overlap with ASL Citizen:** MM-WLAuslan signs whose gloss or keyword matches an ASL Citizen val or test sign by label (597 of 3,215) are left out of training, so lookalike signs don't leak into the evaluation (see findings); they stay in the store. Remaining lookalikes are searched with the embedding model, and the gain on val signs with and without a label match is compared.

- **Resting hands count as undetected** (`PrepConfig.max_hand_y` = 1.0): a hand whose wrist is more than 1 shoulder width below the shoulders is set to missing before trimming and before counting hands for the dominant hand. Close webcam framings like ASL Citizen's cut resting hands off (frame bottom at a median 0.68 shoulder widths below the shoulders), wider ones like MM-WLAuslan's show them; with the rule, trimming and the one-handed rule work alike for both, and a model can't tell the datasets apart by resting hands. It would also cover webcam users whose resting hands are visible. The pose arm landmarks stay (estimated off-frame in ASL Citizen, visible in MM-WLAuslan).
- **Phonological auxiliary targets next** (after MM-WLAuslan): the operating points show that the model rejects random wrong signs well but accepts most attempts of the most similar sign, and similar signs typically differ in one phonological parameter. ASL-LEX labels every ASL Citizen sign with them, is public, and has helped in related work (see findings); the parameters are also largely shared across sign languages (Swedish later). Architecture and regularization have converged, and Sem-Lex (more ASL signs) is still blocked.
- **Signs of another sign language get their own labels** (prefix `auslan:` at preparation), since an Auslan sign is a different sign from the ASL sign with the same English label; within one language, the same label means the same sign across datasets.
- **WLASL is the next source instead of Sem-Lex**, whose download links are dead (see findings). It is the same language, so by the vocabulary scaling its signs should be worth about ten times an Auslan sign, and it is the only accessible ASL source of its size. Its videos come from a mirror because the official links have rotted; the mirror is byte-identical to the official files where those are still served, and to a second, independent mirror (see findings).
- **WLASL labels are mapped onto ASL Citizen's** rather than prefixed like Auslan's, since within one sign language the same label means the same sign: a gloss matching exactly one ASL Citizen gloss after `normalize_label` joins that class (930 glosses, most of the 6,646 matching clips), which also thickens ASL Citizen's ~18 clips per sign. A gloss matching several variants (121, e.g. drink -> DRINK1/DRINK2) is left out of training rather than guessed, because the wrong variant would be label noise exactly among the near-minimal pairs the model already confuses. Glosses matching a val or test sign (334) are left out too, and new glosses only train if the hash split puts them in train (490 of 615), so a held-out sign stays held out as more ASL data arrives. Excluded clips stay in the store, as for MM-WLAuslan.

- **The Swedish lexicon is an evaluation set only, and its sign classes come from same-form groups.** Svenskt teckenspråkslexikon is a dictionary, not a dataset: one recording per entry, no signer ids, and the goal vocabulary itself, so it is never trained on. A single clip per sign would allow no k-shot verification at all, but the lexicon marks entries whose sign form is identical and whose meanings differ ("Teckenformen kan också betyda"), and those are separate recordings, usually by a different model signer. Taking each same-form group as one sign class turns the dictionary into a k = 1 cross-signer benchmark of roughly Slovo's size, at no access cost. Signs outside a group still serve as negatives, so the negative pool stays the full vocabulary.
- **The lexicon is first used as the glossary for self-recorded Swedish queries**, before any lexicon-against-itself benchmark. A recording is never by a lexicon signer, so every trial compares two different people without signer ids, and it is the setting the system is for. Every entry is a glossary sign, not only the sign classes: the whole dictionary is the product's glossary, and every entry is a negative for every other sign.
- **Shown clips stay in the glossary, and every recording result comes twice** (replaces "never show a clip the evaluation scores against"). *Copied* scores against the whole glossary, including the clips the recording was shown: copying the dictionary clip and being checked against it is exactly what a user does, so this is the headline. *Uncopied* leaves out every clip shown for the sign (`references` in the recordings' clips.csv) and asks whether the model knows the sign rather than the one performance copied; the gap between the two is how much of a score is mimicry. Up to 3 clips by different signers are still shown, so a recording copies more than one performance where the glossary allows it (a glossary without signer ids shows one). Uncopied has no trial for a sign with a single clip, which is most lexicon entries. The 17 recordings made before `references` existed were shown 4 glossary clips that their clips.csv doesn't list, so for them both settings are the same (top-1 0.87 at k = 1, as first measured; 0.95 without those 4 clips, see findings).
- **Pseudo signer ids by face clustering** for the lexicon: the protocol requires references by a signer other than the query's, and the lexicon publishes no signer ids. The recordings come from a handful of studio models under identical framing and background, so clustering a face embedding of one frame per clip should recover them; without it a same-form pair recorded by the same model would silently be an easy, same-signer trial.

- **Slovo is used as a held-out cross-language evaluation set**, never as training data. Unlike MM-WLAuslan it has signer ids, so it can be evaluated k-shot with references by other signers (999 of its 1,000 signs have at least 6 signers, median 10). Until now every number came from ASL Citizen alone, so it measures nothing about transfer to another sign language; Slovo gives an independent benchmark of a different language, vocabulary and recording setup (crowdsourced phone video), which is the closest available proxy for the Swedish Sign Language goal. As training data it would be worth little: by the vocabulary scaling and the MM-WLAuslan result (cross-language signs are worth ~10-20% of ASL signs), its 1,000 signs on top of the current 4,790 predict +0.2 to +0.4 points top-1, far inside the confidence intervals.
- **Slovo phrase classes are left out of the benchmark:** the task is validating one sign, and a class that is a whole utterance tests matching a sequence of signs, which is a different and generally easier problem. Left out are classes without a single-word gloss (62 of 1,000, e.g. `С днем рождения`, `время от 0 ночи до 12 дня`; a parenthesized note or a semicolon-separated synonym doesn't count as a second word, so `пока (что)` and `кусок; тонкими слоями` stay) and classes whose median clip takes over 3 s to sign, which catches phrases the label doesn't reveal. They stay in the landmark store.

- **The collection tool never shows a score.** It reports only whether a recording is usable: whether `preparation.prepare_clip` would keep it, and the share of frames with a hand detected between the first and last frame with a hand (0.76-1.00 on ASL Citizen clips, median ~0.98; measured over the whole clip it would be ~0.4 even for a clean recording, because the rest before and after the sign has no hands in view). A visible score would let the signer retake until the model agrees, biasing the set toward clips the model already likes, and the validity check is what keeps a whole session from turning out unusable afterwards.
- **Recordings are extracted on the server with `extraction.py`**, not in the browser with MediaPipe's JavaScript build. A different extractor build would confound the thing the set is meant to measure (a new signer, camera and room) with a change of extractor, against the project's one-fixed-extractor constraint. The cost is ~2 s per clip, which is acceptable for record-then-check. The uploads are transcoded to a constant 30 fps with ffmpeg first: the browser's MediaRecorder writes variable frame rate video, and preparation needs one meaningful frame rate per clip.
- **Reference clips come from several signers** (default 3) rather than one: copying a single clip makes the recording partly a mimicry of that one performance, which would inflate its similarity to that reference. Copying a dictionary clip at all is realistic, since that is what a user of the system does.

- **The embedding encodes the sign, not the gloss.** Words that share one sign must never be separated: not as different training classes pushed apart by the loss, and not as wrong-sign trials in the evaluation. Glosses are dictionary words, and the word-to-sign mapping is many-to-many: one sign is filed under several words (WLASL files one video under habit/tradition, much/a lot) and one form can carry unrelated meanings (CALIFORNIA/GOLD). Merging different signs into one class is the lesser error, but still one to avoid, e.g. by chaining synonyms through a word with several signs (`last` shares videos with both `final` and `past`).
- **ASL Citizen's twins are the glosses that ASL-LEX links to one ASL SignBank entry** (98 pairs, without numbered variants of one gloss). SignBank has one entry per sign form, so this is a linguist's form judgment, unlike the SignBank translation lists, which group a meaning family: CHAIR and SIT have identical lists ("chair, seat, sit, sit down") but are a noun-verb minimal pair with different SignBank entries. Numbered variants (DOG1/DOG2) share an entry because ASL-LEX links each entry to the closest one, but were filmed separately because they are signed differently, so they are not twins. Labeling candidates by video is hard: ASL Citizen's signers perform the same gloss differently often enough to make labels unreliable. Signs without a SignBank link (27%) get no twins. The asl-lex.org videos can't be used: their terms allow only personal searches of the database.
- **Twins are masked from all sources, but held-out leakage is judged per dataset.** Training masks every twin pair (WLASL shared videos and SignBank), since a wrong mask costs little. A WLASL word that is a twin of a held-out sign by either source is left out of training (its clips may show the held-out sign). An ASL Citizen training sign is left out only for a SignBank twin of a held-out sign: WLASL's links reach ASL Citizen through English words, which often land on a different variant or a minimal pair (CATEGORY/GROUP, COOK/KITCHEN, AIRPLANE/FLY, AGAIN/OVER), and would have cost 109 signs (3,321 clips) instead of 37 (1,130).
- **The evaluation doesn't score twins against each other** (`evaluate(..., twins=)`): a query's score for a twin of its sign is neither a negative trial nor a competitor for top-1/top-5. The twins are those of the loaded prepared data, so `evaluate_test.py` (ASL Citizen only) uses the SignBank pairs, while the val evaluation during training also sees WLASL's pairs when WLASL is trained on (4 val pairs instead of 2); comparisons across training sets belong in `evaluate_test.py`.
- **Twin signs are left out of each other's softmax, not merged.** For each training clip, the ArcFace logits of the classes known to be the same sign form (`PreparedData.twins`, so far from WLASL's shared videos) are set to -inf: the loss neither pushes the twins apart nor pulls them together. Merging would chain distinct signs through words with several signs (`over` links AGAIN, ACROSS and END; `last` links FINAL and PAST), and dropping the shared video from one word would leave the words' other clips to push the classes apart. Shared videos stay under every word (each copy anchors its class to the same example). A word that is a twin of a held-out sign is left out of training entirely, since its other clips may show the held-out sign too.
- **The lexicon's resting pose is not part of the sign** (user decision, 2026-09-19): its models rest with the hands clasped at the waist, in frame, which a webcam user's rest never is, and in one-handed signs the idle hand rests there throughout. The lexicon is prepared with `max_hand_y` 0.9 (`prepare_sts_lexikon.py`'s default; the global default stays 1.0, so training data is untouched). Chosen by a sweep over the self-recorded clips (see findings), which beat the earlier proposal of keeping the rest at 1.2. A rest rule that would carry across framings instead of a per-dataset cutoff was tried and rejected (see findings): stillness can't tell a rest from a sign held low with little movement, only height can.
- **The best configuration of the validation search is the training default** (2026-09-19): `TrainConfig` now defaults to hidden 384, 20 epochs, `ema_decay` 0.999, `phonology_weight` 1 with `phonology_input` pooled, and `scripts/train.py` to ASL Citizen + MM-WLAuslan + WLASL, so `uv run scripts/train.py --name <name>` trains it. Earlier runs are unaffected: each run's `config.json` records every setting it used (a run from before `ema_decay` existed loads with the new default, which only matters for training). Re-running an old command without its settings now trains the new configuration.
- **The practice app accepts an attempt at a mean cosine similarity of 0.38 to the sign's clips** (user decision, 2026-09-19, provisional). A yes/no answer needs a threshold, although threshold selection is otherwise out of scope. 0.38 is the equal-error point of the 51 Swedish recordings, extracted in the browser, against all 16,026 lexicon signs with `iv14_h384_e20`: 2.0% of correct attempts rejected, 2.0% of random wrong signs accepted (so an attempt matches about 300 lexicon signs; the same point from the Python extraction is 0.381). Stricter points: 0.45 rejects 5.9% and accepts 0.84%, 0.50 rejects 5.9% and accepts 0.44%, 0.55 rejects 11.8% and accepts 0.23%. Similar signs are accepted more often than random ones (`scripts/operating_points.py` measures that on ASL). It rests on 1 signer and 17 signs, and on one model: retrain or change the preparation, and it must be chosen again.

## Open decisions

- Fingerspelled MM-WLAuslan signs (letters and fingerspelled words): keep or drop. They can't be counted from the landmarks (see findings); the 24 letters are 1% of the Auslan training signs. Proposed: keep.

- Whether the framing difference belongs in training instead. About 29% of the lexicon's signing frames are deeper than ASL Citizen's frame bottom (0.69 shoulder widths), so they are articulations the model has never seen in training, whatever the lexicon's cutoff. Proposed, if the sweep shows it matters: a training augmentation that hides hands below a randomly sampled depth, which makes the model robust to framing generally rather than special-casing one dataset.

## Findings

Kaggle ASL Signs:
- 94,477 clips of 250 signs by 21 signers. Balanced: 299–415 clips per sign, 3.3k–5k clips per signer.
- Hand detections are almost entirely `left_hand` or entirely `right_hand` per signer: 9 left-dominant, 12 right-dominant signers. Explained by the data's origin: PopSign ASL is one-handed smartphone signing, with the other hand presumably holding the phone.
- The hand labels appear to refer to the signer's own hands in an unmirrored image (`left_hand` shows up on the image's right side). Seen in a few clips only.
- 39% of frames have no hand detected; every clip has a hand in at least one frame.
- Sequence length: median 22 frames, 95th percentile 135, max 537. 43 clips have gaps in their frame numbers (closed by the adapter). The capture frame rate is unknown.
- Coordinates go far outside [0, 1] (x from −1.3 to 2.9, y up to 3.6), mostly from out-of-frame lower-body pose landmarks.
- Some clips are very short and seem to capture only part of the sign.

ASL Citizen:
- Single 45.9 GB zip from the Microsoft Download Center, no registration. Commercial use requires contacting ASL_Citizen@microsoft.com.
- License (`use.txt`, Microsoft Research License Terms): non-commercial research only; the data and modifications of it (e.g. extracted landmarks) may not be distributed; personal data must be destroyed when the research is completed.
- Layout: `ASL_Citizen/videos/*.mp4` (83,399 videos, ~50 GB) and `ASL_Citizen/splits/{train,val,test}.csv` with columns `Participant ID`, `Video file`, `Gloss`, `ASL-LEX Code`.
- Official splits are signer-disjoint: 35 / 6 / 11 signers with 40,154 / 10,304 / 32,941 videos. All 2,731 glosses appear in every split; 21–45 videos per gloss (mean 31). Videos per signer are very uneven (2 to 3,004, median 1,496).
- Glosses are cleaned labels (e.g. file `NOT MIND` → gloss `NOTMIND`, `SAIL` → `SAIL1`). 2,723 ASL-LEX codes; only RESEARCH1/2 and WHATFOR1/2/3 share a real code (all in the train split, so no leakage), and a few glosses have code `NA`.
- Videos (sample of 300): mostly H.264 640x480, some 960x540 and MPEG-4. Frame rate varies (mostly ~30 fps, also 25 and 15 fps). Mean length 2.7 s / 80 frames (33–366), so ~6.7M frames in total and a ~44 GB landmark store at float32.

- First extraction test (32 videos): face and pose detected in 100% of frames; `left_hand` in 33%, `right_hand` in 42%, both in 30%, neither in 55% (recordings start and end with the hands down). Two-handed signing is present, unlike Kaggle.
- Hand dropouts (30-sign subset, 932 clips, 76,918 frames): 57% of frames have no hand, of which 53 points are before the first / after the last detected hand (hands down) and 4 points are 948 short gaps during signing. During signing, with the pose wrist clearly inside the image (y < 0.85), the hand is detected in ~91% of frames.
- Extractor comparison on 40 videos (hand detected when the pose wrist is clearly visible, all frames): holistic 83.9%; holistic with hand confidence 0.2 identical (the option has no effect); separate hand + pose landmarkers 83.0%; the same with thresholds 0.3: 84.6% but more hands far from their wrist; image mode (no tracking) 77.6%. No setup meaningfully reduces dropouts; separate hand + pose without face mesh is ~20% cheaper on the CPU.
- Full store (`scripts/explore_store.py`): fps mostly 30 (70,744 clips), then 31 (5,804), 25 (3,173), 15 (1,698). Clip duration median 2.6 s (99%: 6.9 s, max 22.6 s); first to last frame with a hand median 1.2 s (95%: 2.5 s, 99%: 4.0 s, max 19.9 s).
- 57.2% of frames have no hand: 53.1 points before the first / after the last hand, 4.1 points gaps during signing. 109 clips have no hand at all; ~250 clips have hands for under 0.2 s and ~50 for over 10 s (likely broken or multi-attempt recordings).
- Two-handedness is bimodal: ~28% of clips have (almost) no frame with both hands; 54.8% have both hands in at least half of their frames with a hand.
- Dominant hand: unlike Kaggle, no signer is one-sided. For most signers `left_hand` is the more-detected hand in 15–45% of their clips (right-handed); ~10 signers are at 55–80% (left-handed, or mirrored webcam video, which the landmarks cannot tell apart). Counting detected frames per clip is ambiguous for two-handed signs.
- Handedness: in one-handed clips (26,278 with both hands in < 10% of their hand frames) the detected hand's label is on the expected image side in 97% of clips (pose right shoulder on the image's left in 99.95% of frames), and per signer it is clear-cut: 28 signers are ≤ 10% `left_hand`, 7 are ≥ 90%, 12 are in between (possibly mirroring that changed between recording sessions, or switching hands). For the 35 clear-cut signers, the detected hand matches the signer's handedness in 97.2% of one-handed clips, but no per-clip rule works for two-handed clips: which hand moves more (hand wrist path, frames with both hands) matches in only 51.6% (63% when one hand moves 3x more). Pose wrist motion is useless for this: the pose model's estimates for resting, out-of-frame wrists jitter as much as the signing hand moves.
- Suspected broken clips, inspected as video frames: 109 clips without any hand (22 signers, 40 from P18) and 152 with hands for under 0.2 s are mostly extraction failures on real signing (hands in front of the face, raised above the head, blurred or partly out of frame), plus a few truncated recordings; 50 clips with over 10 s of hand activity (47 from P46) are long recordings with idle time, so hand presence cannot locate the sign. All 311 (0.37%) are excluded with the planned thresholds. Hands in front of the face are a weak spot of hand detection.
- Prepared data (default config): train 40,015 clips (2,172 signs), val 5,326 (290), test 3,218 (269); 148 excluded. Prepared length median 41–42 frames, 95th percentile 74–80, max 128. Mirrored: 17% of train and val clips but 44% of test clips, so several of the 11 test signers are left-dominant or recorded mirrored.
- Evaluation harness sanity check on val (290 signs): random embeddings give AUC 0.50, EER 0.50, top-1 0.003 (= 1/290), top-5 0.017 (= 5/290). A trivial hand-crafted embedding (dominant hand's mean shape relative to the wrist, mean wrist position and wrist path at 8 time points) gives AUC 0.83 / 0.88 / 0.89, EER 0.25 / 0.20 / 0.19, top-1 16% / 19% / 20% for k = 1 / 3 / 5; 95% CI for AUC at k = 1: 0.815–0.839. Hardest signs for it: BARK2, END, MISSING, AXE1, BOW2. Trained models have to beat this.
- Baselines without training on val (k = 1 / 3 / 5, 95% CI about ±0.01 for AUC):
  - Hand features (cosine of the hand-crafted embedding): AUC 0.827 / 0.875 / 0.887, EER 0.249 / 0.204 / 0.191, top-1 16.3% / 19.0% / 20.0%.
  - DTW (both hands + upper body, 32 frames, missing hands at the pose wrist): AUC 0.817 / 0.850 / 0.859, EER 0.259 / 0.231 / 0.223, top-1 17.8% / 20.7% / 22.4%. Better at identification but worse at verification than the hand features.
  - The difference is calibration: raw DTW distances vary in scale between queries, which hurts a single global threshold. Z-normalizing each query's similarities (against all other clips of the split, i.e. a cohort of other signs) lifts DTW to AUC 0.832 / 0.877 / 0.890 and EER 0.185 at k = 5, on par with the hand features (0.894, 0.186 normalized), without changing identification. Relevant for threshold selection later; a cohort of other signs' references would be needed at inference.
- GRU + ArcFace baseline (bidirectional 2-layer GRU, hidden 256, 256-d embedding, ArcFace scale 30 / margin 0.3, AdamW 1e-3, 60 epochs of ~9 s on the RTX 3090): val AUC 0.979 / 0.991 / 0.992, EER 0.061 / 0.036 / 0.033, top-1 75.3% / 83.7% / 86.1% for k = 1 / 3 / 5 (95% CI for top-1 at k = 5: 84.0–87.8%), far above the baselines without training (AUC 0.887, top-1 22% at k = 5). Already after 2 epochs: AUC 0.969, top-1 48% (k = 5).
- The GRU overfits: validation peaks around epochs 15–20 (best checkpoint epoch 15) while training accuracy keeps rising to 99.7% at epoch 60 and validation slowly declines. With ~18 training clips per sign, more regularization, augmentation or data should help.
- Validation AUC is close to saturated (0.99 with CIs of ±0.002), so EER and top-1, especially at k = 1, are the more sensitive metrics for comparing models. Validation signers are also training signers, so the test split will show how much of this carries over to unseen signers.
- Regularization experiments (GRU, 30 epochs, val EER / top-1 at k = 1): defaults 0.0593 / 75.7%; dropout 0.4 + weight decay 0.05: 0.0605 / 75.6%; stronger augmentation (rotation 20°, scale 0.3, shift 0.15, shear 0.2, speed 0.7–1.4, frame drop 0.2, hand drop 0.25): 0.0583 / 75.8%; both: 0.0575 / 75.9%. All within noise (CI about ±0.006 EER, ±2 points top-1). Regularization does reduce the train/val gap (train accuracy 92% instead of 98% at epoch 30) and keeps validation at its best level through epoch 30 instead of degrading, but the best validation result doesn't improve: overfitting is not what limits the GRU; the architecture or the amount of data is.
- Conv + transformer vs GRU (new defaults, 30 epochs, val EER / top-1 at k = 1): GRU 0.0575 / 75.9% (2.3M parameters, 9 s/epoch); conv + transformer width 192, 2 stacks 0.0676 / 73.2% (1.2M, 7 s/epoch); width 256, 3 stacks 0.0651 / 73.4% (3.0M, 11 s/epoch); width 384, 2 stacks 0.0680 / 73.0% (4.5M, 12 s/epoch). Size makes no difference; the transformers still improve until epoch 22–30 and reach only 86–89% training accuracy, which points to undertraining rather than overfitting.
- Conv + transformer follow-ups (width 192, val EER / top-1 at k = 1 / top-1 at k = 5): 80 epochs 0.0657 / 74.0% / 85.0%, peaking around epoch 26–30 and declining afterwards (not undertrained); dropout 0.2 instead of 0.4: 0.0621 / 75.3% / 86.4%, within noise of the GRU (0.0575 / 75.9% / 86.1%). Dropout 0.4 was too strong for the transformer.
- Across 11 runs, different architectures (GRU, conv + transformer, 1.2–4.5M parameters) and regularization settings converge to about the same level (EER ≈ 0.058–0.062, top-1 ≈ 76% at k = 1), which suggests the models are limited by the data (2,172 training signs, ~18 clips each, 40 signers) rather than by the architecture.
- Unseen signers: the GRU (current defaults) retrained without 7 training signers (P16, P14, P51, P30, P23, P7, P43; 20% of the training clips) vs trained on all (`gru_reg_aug`), on the val signs. On the 7 held-out signers' clips (1,071) EER / top-1 at k = 1 go from 0.0605 / 75.6% to 0.0654 / 73.6%; on the other signers' clips (4,255), which only see the smaller training set, from 0.0568 / 76.0% to 0.0586 / 74.7%. Signer gap: +0.003 EER and −0.7 points top-1 at k = 1 (−1.3 points at k = 5), within noise (CIs about ±0.01 EER, ±2.5 points top-1 per group). The validation numbers are thus hardly inflated by signer overlap, more signers alone are unlikely to help much, and 20% less training data costs only ~1 point; more signs (vocabulary) is the more promising lever, so the overlap of Sem-Lex's vocabulary with ASL Citizen's matters.
- **A reference by the query's own signer makes a trial much easier**, which is a different question from the signer gap above (that one asks whether the model generalizes to people it never trained on; this one asks what a same-person reference pair does at evaluation time). Same-person repeats of a sign are common: 17% of ASL Citizen clips and 65% of Slovo clips. Measured with `gru_auslan_phonpool1`, paired over queries that have both a same-signer and a different-signer clip of their sign, against the same negatives: mean cosine to the positive 0.779 vs 0.671 on the ASL Citizen test split (621 queries) and 0.746 vs 0.620 on Slovo (12,092); the same-signer clip scores higher for 76% and 78% of queries; EER at k = 1 drops from 0.0499 to 0.0420 (−16%) and from 0.0466 to 0.0268 (−42%). The protocol's rule that references come from other signers is therefore doing real work in every number recorded so far, and an evaluation set without signer ids would be optimistic by a comparable, unknown amount — which is why the lexicon needs pseudo signer ids before its numbers mean anything.
- Vocabulary scaling (GRU, current defaults, same number of training steps, val EER / top-1 at k = 1): 543 signs (10k clips) 0.0766 / 64.2%; 1,086 (20k) 0.0658 / 70.1%; 1,629 (30k) 0.0617 / 73.9%; 2,172 (40k) 0.0575 / 75.9% (CIs about ±2.3 points top-1). Log-linear: each doubling of the training vocabulary gives about +6 points top-1 and −0.009 EER at k = 1 (+5.5 points, −0.0065 at k = 5, flattening slightly). Unlike architecture and regularization, more signs (with their clips) is a real lever; for comparison, 20% fewer clips of the same signs (signer holdout) cost only ~1.3 points. Extrapolated, doubling the vocabulary with Sem-Lex could give roughly +4–6 points top-1 at k = 1, depending on how many of its signs are new.
- Kaggle ASL Signs vocabulary vs ASL Citizen: 208 of its 250 signs match an ASL Citizen gloss by label (ignoring case, sense numbers and punctuation); most of the other 42 are the same signs under different labels (e.g. dad/FATHER, mom/MOTHER). Kaggle would add many clips for existing signs but few new signs.
- Hardest val signs for the GRU (k = 5, by AUC): CHEERLEADER2, BOW2, SKATEBOARDING3, EGO, CRAWL1, FAN, LONGWORD, LUNCH1.
- Frame size (backfilled from the videos' first decoded frame): 80,184 clips 640x480, 3,211 at 960x540, 4 at 480x640.
- Signers: 26 of 52 recorded nearly the whole vocabulary (~3,000 clips each); P13 and P19 have 2 clips each.
- Coordinates (0.1–99.9 percentiles): hands x −0.02–1.03, y −0.01–1.12; upper body x −0.1–1.14, y 0.17–1.8 (arms below the frame when the hands are down). `right_hand` lies mostly on the image's left (x 0.08–0.76 at 1–99%), consistent with the Kaggle hand label convention.
- Visual inspection of missed hands during signing: motion blur (most common), overlapping or touching hands, and hands seen edge-on. These are limits of the footage and hand models, not of the setup, so the holistic extractor is kept and gaps are handled in the loader.

WLASL:
- The official repository (https://github.com/dxli94/WLASL, Dongxu Li, WACV 2020) distributes metadata and a downloader, not videos. `start_kit/WLASL_v0.3.json` (12 MB) holds the 21,083 instances of 2,000 glosses, each with its gloss, signer id, frame range, bounding box in the source frame and source url. License C-UDA: academic and computational use only.
- Its video links have rotted: 5,135 instances point to YouTube (where yt-dlp now needs cookies against bot checks) and 15,948 to 19 ASL dictionary sites. Of 40 sampled direct urls (2026-09) 23 answered 200, 7 gave 403, 8 gave 406 and 2 did not connect; retried with a browser User-Agent, aslu, asl5200 and asllex answer 200, but aslpro gives 404, handspeak 404 for 3 of 4, signingsavvy stays 403 and aslsignbank is unreachable. Those four sources alone are 7,686 instances, 36% of the dataset. The authors send links to the missing videos on request (a Google form, answered "within 7 days"), which is the way to fill the gap.
- The videos therefore come from the 2021 mirror `risangbaskoro/wlasl-processed` on Kaggle (4.8 GB in one archive, includes WLASL's own metadata and a `missing.txt` listing the 9,103 ids it lacks (without a final newline, so `wc -l` shows 9,102), so 11,980 remain). It preserves clips whose sources are unreachable today (07074 is a signingsavvy clip). Its files keep the native source resolution (288x192 to 1920x1080, each matching the coordinate space of that instance's bbox) and are already trimmed to one sign, so they look like the official preprocessing rather than a re-encode. The Hugging Face mirror Voxel51/WLASL has the same videos as single files, but 11,880 requests run into HTTP 429 rate limiting.
- The mirror's 9,103 missing ids are every YouTube instance (5,135), 2,211 handspeak, 1,736 aslpro (Flash `.swf` files) and 21 aslsignbank ones. Probing 40 random missing urls per source (2026-09-19, browser User-Agent, YouTube through its oEmbed endpoint): YouTube 30 still public, 7 removed or private, 3 answered 403; aslpro and handspeak 404 for all 40; aslsignbank unreachable. `uvx yt-dlp` resolves a public one without cookies, so roughly 3,800 YouTube clips look downloadable by ourselves, while the dictionary-site clips can only come from the authors. The YouTube instances would still need trimming to their `frame_start`/`frame_end`, since the adapter expects clips of one sign like the mirror's.
- Downloading them (2026-09-19, 4 parallel downloads, ~2 videos/s): a video's frame count matches WLASL's frame numbers exactly (XB3Q6IX6CBo: 11,498 frames, its last instance ends at frame 11,498), and the clips keep the native frame rate (mostly 29.97 fps, up to 1080p). After 193 videos in 97 s YouTube answered every request with "Sign in to confirm you're not a bot", even a single request right afterwards, so the download needs to go much slower or with a signed-in session's cookies.
- Vocabulary against ASL Citizen (`normalize_label`, also ignoring spaces): 615 of the 2,000 glosses are new, 1,051 match a training sign, 334 match a val or test sign. Among the mirror's clips that is 3,084, 6,646 and 2,150 clips. 930 of the 1,051 training matches are unambiguous; 121 match several ASL Citizen variants (drink -> DRINK1/DRINK2, cool -> COOL1..COOL4). The 615 new glosses fall 490 / 62 / 63 into train / val / test by the hash split.
- The sources are ASL dictionary and lesson sites (signingsavvy, signschool, asldeafined, aslsearch, ...), so the clips are mostly single professional signers in studio framings rather than ASL Citizen's webcam recordings; 78 signer ids appear among the available clips.
- The store keeps WLASL's own glosses and the mapping to ASL Citizen's labels happens in preparation, so in `scripts/view_clips.py` a clip is found by `--sign yesterday` against the store but by `--sign YESTERDAY` against the prepared directory.
- **Synonym glosses share videos.** WLASL files one dictionary video under several English glosses (rain/precipitation, much/a lot, habit/tradition, go/leave/attend): of the 11,980 mirrored clips, 1,792 come from a source url that carries more than one gloss (804 urls), and 1,883 clips are exact landmark duplicates of another clip. Two consequences for the current preparation: (1) label noise, since the same video trains two classes that ArcFace pushes apart; (2) leakage of held-out signs, since 258 of those urls pair a train gloss with a val or test gloss, so 328 of the 8,384 WLASL training clips are the very video of a held-out sign under another label (e.g. `tradition` trains while HABIT is a test sign). `gru_wlasl`'s val and test numbers are therefore slightly optimistic; its conclusion (no gain on ASL Citizen) only gets stronger. Its Slovo numbers are unaffected. No two clips of the same gloss share a video under different signer ids, so within one class the duplicates don't inflate k-shot scores.
- The shared videos also point at possible leakage inside ASL Citizen's own split: mapping the WLASL words to ASL Citizen labels, 193 twin pairs link a training sign to a val or test sign, and 98 of the training signs are ASL Citizen classes (2,986 of its 83,399 clips), e.g. NOW/TODAY, WEIGH/WEIGHT, DUE/OWE, CUT/WOOD, SOUP/SPOON, HOP/JUMP. The evidence is weaker than for WLASL itself: it rests on an English word matching the ASL Citizen gloss, and a word with several signs can link to the wrong one (LAST/PAST comes from `last` meaning *previously*, while ASL Citizen's LAST may be the *final* sign). Not acted on yet; checking such pairs in ASL Citizen's own clips belongs with the same-form search there.
- ASL-LEX's SignBank links (`SignBankAnnotationID`) cover 2,005 of ASL Citizen's 2,731 signs; the other 726 also lack SignBank translation lists. 147 pairs of ASL Citizen signs share an entry, 49 of them numbered variants of one gloss (DOG1/DOG2, EAT1/EAT2); of the other 98, 58 are two training signs, 37 a training and a held-out sign (DEATH/DIE, HURT/PAIN, BLEED/BLOOD, PITY/SYMPATHY, MARCH/PARADE), 3 two held-out signs (CREATE/MAKE and DONTMIND/NOTMIND in val, HONEST/TRUTH across test and val). A few look like distinct forms (CHOPSTICKS/SCISSORS, ASSIST/ASSISTANT). Translation lists are looser: 2,006 pairs where one list names the other's gloss, 233 where both do; the mutual pairs still include noun-verb pairs (CHAIR/SIT, CAR/DRIVE). WLASL's shared videos link 127 pairs of ASL Citizen glosses with different SignBank entries, some genuine twins (ART/DRAW, BOX/ROOM, CALIFORNIA/GOLD), others minimal pairs or polysemy chains (CATEGORY/GROUP, COOK/KITCHEN, AGAIN/OVER).
- ASL Citizen's participants recorded each sign after watching a seed signer's video, and the seed signer is one of its 52 participants; P48, the only participant with all 2,731 signs (all in the official test split), is probably the seed signer (not verified).
- ASL Citizen twin candidates (`scripts/find_twins.py`): 1,324 pairs, 142 from WLASL, 1,055 from ASL-LEX, 296 from the similarity top 150 of each embedding; 27 pairs have all four signals, 140 three. Two hubs had to be removed first: an inconsistent sign (WHAT1) looks like everything's twin if the gap is taken against the lower of the two signs' own similarities (now the higher), and generic SignBank translations ("animal", "time") link dozens of signs (words listed by more than 3 entries are now ignored). The top of the list is dominated by known same-form ASL pairs (FRANCE/FRENCH, KILL/MURDER, ART/DRAW, BOX/ROOM, INFECTION/INSURANCE, EQUAL/EVEN/FAIR, EARMUFFS/HEADPHONES, ANTLERS/MOOSE), the model's gap for them is close to 0 (as alike as each sign is with itself), and many cross the split: among the 167 pairs with three or four signals, 47 pair a training sign with a held-out one (DEATH/DIE, PITY/SYMPATHY, SOUP/SPOON, NOW/TODAY, FUTURE/WILL, MARCH/PARADE) and 8 are two held-out signs scored as different signs (CREATE/MAKE in val, COST/PRICE and TO/UNTIL in test, HONEST/TRUTH). Not yet verified by eye.
- Training comparison (`gru_wlasl` = `gru_auslan_phonpool1` + WLASL, 100k training clips): no gain. At the selected checkpoints (epoch 12 vs 24) val EER / top-1 0.0548 / 77.2% vs 0.0538 / 78.5% at k = 1, 0.0299 / 87.0% vs 0.0276 / 88.5% at k = 5; paired over the 290 val signs, top-1 changes by −1.3 points at k = 1 (95% CI −2.0 to −0.6), −1.3 at k = 3, −1.4 at k = 5, EER by +0.2 points at k = 3 and 5. The light per-epoch checks of both runs are within noise of each other from epoch 14 on (top-1 at k = 1 ~78.5% at epoch 30 for both), so part of the loss is the earlier checkpoint that validation EER happened to select. The vocabulary scaling predicted about +1.8 points top-1 for the 490 new signs (0.29 doublings), so WLASL clips are worth far less than ASL Citizen clips. Candidate reasons, untested: ~5 clips per new gloss by 1–3 signers, the dictionary-site domain (framings, resolutions, frame rates), and label noise from the gloss mapping. Epochs took ~39 s instead of ~19 s, more than the 9% more clips explain.
- On Slovo (`scripts/evaluate_test.py gru_wlasl --prepared data/prepared/slovo-d314433a --name slovo`) the same model is better than `gru_auslan_phonpool1`, most clearly in verification at k = 1: EER / top-1 / top-5 0.0439 / 52.8% / 81.5% vs 0.0490 / 52.0% / 80.4% at k = 1, 0.0247 / 63.8% / 90.8% vs 0.0263 / 63.6% / 90.6% at k = 3, 0.0214 / 67.1% / 92.7% vs 0.0219 / 67.2% / 92.7% at k = 5. Paired over the 935 signs, EER changes by −0.53 points at k = 1 (95% CI −0.66 to −0.41), −0.20 at k = 3, −0.13 at k = 5, and top-1 by +0.9 points at k = 1 (+0.3 to +1.4), within noise at k = 3 and 5. So the WLASL clips don't help on ASL Citizen's own recording setup but make the embedding transfer better to an unseen language and setup, which fits their different domain (dictionary sites, many framings) and matters more for the deployment and Swedish goals than val does. The val set only measures ASL Citizen-style recordings, so checkpoint selection on val EER may also undervalue such runs.
- With the twins masked and held-out leakage removed, WLASL no longer hurts on ASL Citizen and still helps on Slovo. `gru_wlasl_twins` vs `gru_auslan_twins` (the same re-prepared data without WLASL), k = 1, EER / top-1: val 0.0540 / 78.9% vs 0.0556 / 78.3% (the val twins differ slightly, 4 pairs vs 2); test 0.0457 / 75.7% vs 0.0452 / 75.7%, paired top-1 gain +0.03 points at k = 1 (95% CI −0.7 to +0.8), +0.3 at k = 3, +0.2 at k = 5, all within noise, where `gru_wlasl` lost −0.9 and −1.1 points at k = 3 and 5 (CIs excluding 0); Slovo 0.0456 / 53.4% vs 0.0501 / 51.4%, paired top-1 gain +2.0 points at k = 1 (+1.5 to +2.5), +1.0 at k = 3, +0.7 at k = 5 (CIs excluding 0). So the synonym-video noise was what cost `gru_wlasl` on ASL Citizen. The SignBank twins on their own (37 signs dropped, 56 pairs masked, and a different training run, so seed noise included) changed nothing on test (`gru_auslan_twins` vs `gru_auslan_phonpool1`: top-1 −0.1, 0.0, +0.1 points) and cost a little on Slovo at k = 1 (−0.6 points, CI −1.1 to −0.2).
- On the ASL Citizen test split (269 signs by 11 unseen signers) `gru_wlasl` vs `gru_auslan_phonpool1`, EER / top-1: 0.0450 / 75.6% vs 0.0456 / 75.9% at k = 1, 0.0253 / 85.0% vs 0.0243 / 86.0% at k = 3, 0.0226 / 87.2% vs 0.0215 / 88.3% at k = 5. Paired over signs, EER is unchanged at every k (k = 1: −0.17 points, 95% CI −0.44 to +0.11) and top-1 changes by −0.2 points at k = 1 (−1.2 to +0.7), −0.9 at k = 3 (−1.9 to −0.1), −1.0 at k = 5 (−2.0 to −0.2). Unseen ASL Citizen signers thus side with val: on ASL Citizen's recording setup WLASL is neutral for verification and slightly negative for identification, and its gain shows only on Slovo's different setup.

Slovo:
- Single ~16 GB zip from the authors' object storage, no registration; license a variant of CC BY-SA 4.0. Layout: `train/<attachment id>.mp4` (15,300), `test/<attachment id>.mp4` (5,100) and a tab-separated `annotations.csv` with columns `attachment_id`, `text` (the Russian label), `user_id`, `height`, `width`, `length` and `train`. The videos are already trimmed to the gesture; the `begin`/`end` frame columns of the documentation belong to the untrimmed 105 GB release.
- 20,400 clips: 1,000 signs with exactly 20 clips each, plus 400 `no_event` clips (no signing). 194 signers, very unevenly: median 13 clips per signer, max 3,692. Mostly portrait phone video (1080x1920 for half the clips, 45 different frame sizes in all); 50 frames on average (median 47, max 270).
- Usable k-shot: 999 of the 1,000 signs have at least 6 distinct signers (median 10, min 4), and one signer contributes a median of 4 of a sign's 20 clips, so k = 1, 3 and 5 references by different signers than the query's all work. Slovo's own train/test split is not signer-disjoint (92 of its 174 test signers also appear in train), which doesn't matter as we never train on it.
- Prepared (`slovo-d314433a`): 935 of the 1,000 signs kept (62 phrase labels and 3 long classes left out, `дятел`, `сторона` and `миграция`), 18,563 of 18,700 clips (137, or 0.7%, excluded as broken, in line with the other datasets), 189 signers, median 45 prepared frames (1.5 s, against ASL Citizen's 41).
- Cross-language transfer of `gru_auslan_phonpool1` (935 RSL signs, 18,563 clips, 189 signers, never trained on), EER / top-1 / top-5: 0.0490 / 52.0% / 80.4% at k = 1, 0.0263 / 63.6% / 90.6% at k = 3, 0.0219 / 67.2% / 92.7% at k = 5; AUC 0.988 at k = 1, 0.997 at k = 5. The verification metrics are the same as on the ASL Citizen test split (0.0456 / 0.0243 / 0.0215 EER, AUC 0.989 / 0.997), so the embedding separates a sign from other signs just as well in a sign language, vocabulary and recording setup it has never seen — the strongest evidence so far for the Swedish Sign Language goal. The identification metrics are not comparable across the two sets: Slovo offers 935 candidate signs against the test split's 269, so they were repeated on 5 random 269-sign subsets of Slovo (`--signs 269`, mean over draws): top-1 / top-5 68.3% / 89.2% at k = 1, 79.7% / 96.1% at k = 3, 82.4% / 97.1% at k = 5, with EER unchanged (0.0530 / 0.0274 / 0.0234). At a matched vocabulary the cross-language loss is 7.6 points of top-1 at k = 1 and 5.9 at k = 5, not the 24 points the raw numbers suggest: two thirds of the apparent gap is the larger candidate set.
- The dominant hand is on the left in 39% of the clips, against ~10% for ASL Citizen, and it is consistent within a signer for 78% of the signers (the second largest signer, 3,264 clips, is 97% left). So it is the signers' front cameras mirroring the video rather than left-handedness, and the preparation's dominant-hand mirroring absorbs it.
- Some classes are phrases, not single signs: 63 of the 1,000 labels are multi-word, and those run ~30% longer than the rest (median 61 vs 46.5 frames). They mix genuine single signs whose Russian gloss needs several words (`летучая мышь` bat, `восход солнца` sunrise, `паучья сеть` spiderweb) with real multi-sign utterances (`время от 0 ночи до 12 дня`, 155 frames; `С днем рождения`; `в восемь пятнадцать`; `две/три/четыре тысячи`). The label only reveals declared phrases: `сторона` ("side") has an ordinary one-word label and the longest clips of all (median 194 frames), so a duration rule is needed as well (see decisions).

Other datasets (surveyed 2026-09; for more training signs, where signs of other sign languages are all new classes):
- **NationalCSL-DP** (Chinese National Sign Language, Scientific Data 2025) is the largest isolated vocabulary found anywhere: 6,707 signs (the whole CNSL vocabulary), 134,140 clips by 10 signers (8 deaf) from a front and a left camera, pre-segmented one clip per sign per signer per view, so 10 front clips per sign. Studio recordings on a green screen in black T-shirts, hands resting at the sides at the start and end like MM-WLAuslan (covered by `max_hand_y`). Public on figshare without registration (https://figshare.com/articles/media/NationalCSL-DP/27261843, DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.27261843, CC BY 4.0, 18.6 GB in 23 files): ten `Participant_NN.zip` of 1.4–1.9 GB with the extracted frames (`Participant_NN/{front,left}/<4-digit sign id>/00001.jpg`, 256x256), ten of 240–345 MB with ~20 raw sample videos each, `gloss.csv` (the 6,707 ids with the Chinese word and an English translation, so label matching against ASL Citizen val/test signs works as for Auslan), and the Java frame extractor. Per participant zip: 6,707 front + 6,707 left clips, 13 / 20 / 27 frames per clip (5th / 50th / 95th percentile, min 5, max 60).
- NationalCSL-DP's catch is the released frames: about 5 fps and low resolution. The raw videos are 1920x1080 at 50 fps (one sample sign video is 3.36 s, 168 frames), but the released frames of the same clip number only 17, i.e. roughly every tenth frame; the bundled extractor keeps every frame of its input and center-crops the 1080x1080 square to 256x256, so the frames come from already-subsampled clips. In the frames a hand is only ~20 px wide (ASL Citizen: 640x480 and much tighter framing) and 5 fps against ASL Citizen's 30 fps loses movement, the parameter the model separates least. So landmark quality has to be measured on one participant zip (hand detection rate during signing, viewer check) before committing to the extraction. The full raw videos (~1.8 TB) need a release agreement signed by full-time staff, students explicitly not accepted, plus email verification (http://lise.lsnu.edu.cn/kxyj/dbxcg2.htm), so the CC BY frames are the realistic option.
- **Slovo** (Russian SL): 1,000 signs, 20,400 FullHD videos, 194 crowdworkers with a `user_id` per clip, public. Of all the extra datasets the closest domain match to ASL Citizen, and the signer ids allow k-shot evaluation on it; in use as the cross-language evaluation set (see the Slovo findings above).
- **V-LIBRASIL** (Brazilian SL): 1,364 signs by 3 signers, 4,089 videos (3 per sign), ~10.5 GB, public on IEEE DataPort and Kaggle. Cheap +1,364 classes, but only 3 clips and 3 signers per sign; its term list was translated from ASLLVD, so the labels (not necessarily the signs) overlap ASL.
- **DEVISIGN** (Chinese SL): 4,414 signs, 331,050 clips, 30 signers, Kinect RGB plus depth, but only the subsets DEVISIGN-G (letters and digits), -D (500 signs) and -L (2,000 signs, 8 signers) are released, to universities and research institutes on request (https://vipl.ict.ac.cn/homepage/ksl/data.html). Same language as NationalCSL-DP, so largely redundant if that one is used.
- ASL: **ASLLVD** (>3,300 signs in citation form, ~9,800 tokens by 1–6 signers each, multi-view, via the Rutgers DAI SignBank) would add ASL signs beyond ASL Citizen's 2,731 and extra clips for existing ones; WLASL (2,000 signs, 21k clips) and MS-ASL (1,000 signs, 25.5k clips, 200+ signers) are YouTube-based with much link rot. For any ASL source the labels must be normalized against ASL Citizen so the hash split keeps held-out signs held out.
- Too small to be worth the pipeline work by the vocabulary scaling, in decreasing order of vocabulary: LSFB-isol (Belgian French SL, 395 signs, 47.5k clips, ~85–100 signers, CC BY-NC, direct download), BosphorusSign22k (Turkish, 744 signs, 6 signers), KArSL (Arabic, 502 signs, 3 signers, Kinect), NMFs-CSL and SLR500 (Chinese, 1,067 and 500 signs, subsumed by NationalCSL-DP), JSL-DC (Japanese, 270 words, 36.7k videos, 19 signers, CC BY 4.0), INCLUDE (Indian, 263 signs), AUTSL (Turkish, 226 signs, 43 signers), SIGMA-ASL (160 ASL signs, but RGB-D, mmWave radar and IMUs), LSA64 (Argentinian, 64 signs). PopSign ASL v1.0, the full set behind Kaggle ASL Signs (250 signs, 175k reviewed clips, 47 Deaf signers), adds clips but hardly any signs and is one-handed smartphone signing.
- JSL-DC is still interesting for another reason: its 270 words were chosen with Deaf and Coda linguists and come with written descriptions that distinguish confusable sign pairs, which is the weakness the operating points show (near-minimal pairs), so it is a possible probe set rather than training data.
- An Indian Sign Language set of 2,002 signs and 40,033 videos by 20 deaf signers (arXiv 2407.14224) is the largest candidate after NationalCSL-DP, but no download link or license is published; it would take an email to the authors.
- SignNet-1M (arXiv 2606.24361, https://signnet.chatsign.ai/) is not new signs but augmented variants of existing ASL, CSL and DGS data (novel views by Gaussian splatting, identity and scene edits by diffusion) — a possible augmentation reference, not a data source.
- There is no up-to-date catalogue: the survey "Sign-Language Datasets at Scale" (arXiv 2606.19352) tabulates the isolated datasets above 500 signs (MS-ASL, WLASL, ASL Citizen, LSFB-isol, DEVISIGN, SLR500, NMFs-CSL, MM-WLAuslan, BosphorusSign22k, AUTSL) but lists neither Sem-Lex nor NationalCSL-DP. Continuous corpora (BOBSL, CSL-Daily, YouTube-ASL, Isharah) stay out of scope, since they are not segmented into isolated signs.
- Nothing found adds ASL signers at scale; Sem-Lex (41 signers) remains the only substantial extra ASL source with both many signs and many signers.
- Dictionaries with one video per sign (reference sets, not training data): ASL-LEX, ASL SignBank, the Swedish lexicon.
- ASL-LEX 2.0 (2,723 signs, the lexicon ASL Citizen's glosses are coded against): public on OSF (https://osf.io/zpha4/, CC BY 4.0, no registration); `Data Files/signdata.csv` (2 MB) with lexical and phonological properties per sign and `signdataKEY.csv` describing the columns; the phonological coding is documented in `Documentation/Supplementary_Materials_Phonological_Coding.docx`.
- Related work on phonology in sign recognition (from the abstracts): predicting ASL-LEX features such as handshape alongside the sign gave nearly +9 points accuracy on WLASL from pose, across architectures (Kezar, Thomason, Sehyr, EACL 2023, "Improving Sign Recognition with Phonology"); on Sem-Lex, +6% few-shot and +2% overall recognition accuracy (Kezar et al. 2023, "The Sem-Lex Benchmark"); with disjoint training and test vocabularies on Sem-Lex poses, an ASL-LEX auxiliary loss plus separate hand, face and movement streams in a VQ autoencoder raised unseen-sign recognition MRR from 0.381 to 0.452 (Kezar, Sehyr, Thomason 2025, arXiv 2509.04745). Minimal-pair probes show pose-based models are sensitive to handshape contrasts and pixel-based models to location (Yin et al., CogSci 2026, arXiv 2606.28667). Not found: auxiliary phonology for k-shot verification with an embedding model; the published gains are over weaker baselines.
- ASL-LEX and ASL Citizen: 2,725 of the 2,731 glosses have an ASL-LEX code (6 are coded `NA`: SENTENCE1, LETTER2, ABOVE, WELCOME1, MINIMUM1, INSTINCT); ASL-LEX has 17 phonological features of the first morpheme (plus a derived MarkedHandshape), from binary ones (contact, wrist twist, repeated movement) to handshape (58 values) and minor location (37). The CSV is Latin-1 encoded.
- Confusions of `gru_auslan` (val, k = 1 top-1 errors, 5 draws) vs random sign pairs: confused pairs differ in fewer ASL-LEX features (median 8 of 17 vs 11; at most 2 differ in 6.4% vs 0.2%), so the embedding already reflects coarse phonology; sign type and major location differ in confusions about half as often as in random pairs (ratios 0.56, 0.62), but movement (0.95), wrist twist (0.93), handshape (0.86), flexion and minor location (0.84) almost as often: these are what the model separates least. The most frequent confusions are close variants and synonyms (GUN2/GUN1, CREATE/MAKE, MEDITATE1/MEDITATE3, MOVIE1/MOVIE2, LIGHT/LAMP2).
- Operating points on val at 5% false rejects (`scripts/operating_points.py`; 107 near-minimal val sign pairs, whose ASL-LEX features differ in at most 2, among 116 signs; only 1 is a numbered variant of one gloss, the rest are different signs like ALONE/CIRCLE, DEAF2/HEAR1, CUP/DIG): `gru_hide_low` accepts 8.1% of random wrong signs, 84% of the most similar sign and 41% of near-minimal pairs at k = 1 (1.2% / 63% / 18% at k = 5); `gru_auslan` 7.1% / 82% / 39% (1.0% / 57% / 17%). Val is harder than test here (test at k = 1: 4.1% random).
- Phonological auxiliary loss on the embedding (`gru_auslan_phon<w>`, weight w for the mean cross-entropy over the 17 features; val, vs `gru_auslan`): EER / top-1 at k = 1 0.0534 / 77.8% (w = 0.3), 0.0529 / 77.9% (w = 1), 0.0540 / 77.6% (w = 3) vs 0.0559 / 77.3%; paired top-1 gain at k = 1 +0.5 (CI −0.1 to +1.1), +0.7 (+0.0 to +1.3), +0.3 points; at k = 5 +0.1, +0.2, −0.6 (−1.2 to −0.0). At 5% false rejects and k = 1, random wrong signs are accepted less (5.9% [5.8, 6.1] for w = 0.3, 6.2% for w = 1, 6.5% for w = 3 vs 7.1% [6.9, 7.4]), the most similar sign about as often (80–83% vs 82%), but near-minimal pairs more often, the more so the higher the weight: 40.9%, 45.9%, 54.9% [50.4, 59.5] vs 38.5% [34.2, 42.9] (k = 5: 18.3%, 20.6%, 28.2% vs 16.8%). The loss organizes the embedding by phonology: signs sharing most features are pulled together (a near-minimal pair shares at least 15 of 17), which helps reject unrelated signs but makes signs that differ in one feature harder to tell apart, the opposite of the intended effect on similar signs. A small weight (0.3) is a modest net gain for rejecting completely wrong signs, the stated priority.
- The same auxiliary loss on the pooled encoder output instead of the embedding (`phonology_input=pooled`, so the embedding layer stays free to separate signs) is better on every metric than on the embedding: val EER / top-1 at k = 1 0.0538 / 78.5% (w = 1) and 0.0545 / 78.5% (w = 3), top-1 at k = 5 88.5% / 87.9%; paired top-1 gain over `gru_auslan` +1.2 points at k = 1 (CI +0.5 to +1.9, both weights) and +0.8 at k = 5 (+0.3 to +1.4) for w = 1, against +0.7 / +0.2 for the embedding heads at w = 1. At 5% false rejects and k = 1 it accepts 6.4% of random wrong signs (vs 7.1% without the loss) and 42.0% of near-minimal pairs (vs 38.5% without, 45.9% with embedding heads): the regression on near-minimal pairs is smaller but not gone. `gru_auslan_phonpool1` is the best model so far: top-1 76.2% -> 78.5% at k = 1 over the ASL-only `gru_hide_low`, half of it from MM-WLAuslan and half from the auxiliary loss.
- Test split (second use): `gru_auslan_phonpool1` EER / top-1 0.0456 / 75.9% at k = 1, 0.0243 / 86.0% at k = 3, 0.0215 / 88.3% at k = 5 (`gru_auslan` 0.0460 / 74.8%, 0.0244 / 85.5%, 0.0223 / 87.7%; `gru_hide_low` 0.0490 / 73.2%, 0.0263 / 83.8%, 0.0229 / 86.6%); AUC unchanged. Paired top-1 gain over `gru_auslan` +1.0 points at k = 1 (CI +0.1 to +1.9), +0.5 at k = 3 and k = 5 (CIs include 0); over `gru_hide_low` +2.6 at k = 1 (+1.6 to +3.6), +2.1 at k = 3, +1.7 at k = 5 (+0.8 to +2.6). At 5% false rejects and k = 1 (118 near-minimal test pairs among 113 signs): random wrong signs accepted 3.9% (4.1%, 4.7%), the most similar sign 71% (72%, 74%), near-minimal pairs 31.4% (29.6%, 30.1%; CIs about ±4.5 points), so on test the near-minimal regression is within noise. The auxiliary loss gain carries over to unseen signers.
- Related work: "Representing Signs as Signs" (Vandendriessche et al., Ghent University, 2025, arXiv 2502.20171) trains an embedding model on MediaPipe pose and hand landmarks of ASL Citizen and recognizes signs one-shot in a Flemish Sign Language dictionary of 10,235 signs: recall@1 0.374, recall@5 0.670, MRR 0.508; trained on a Flemish corpus with 292 signs instead, recall@1 is only 0.089. Supports both the vocabulary finding and cross-language transfer (relevant for Swedish Sign Language later).

- 30-sign sample of Valid (60 clips, 4,619 frames): clips 1.0–4.1 s (median 2.6 s). Unlike ASL Citizen, the hands are in the frame when resting (wrist y ≈ 0.8–0.95; shoulders at y ≈ 0.5), so hands are detected in 92% of frames (ASL Citizen: 43%), only 2% of frames lie before the first or after the last hand, and in one-handed signs the idle hand is detected, holding still, throughout. Consequences for preparation: trimming to the frames with hands removes nothing (clips keep the rest at start and end); 92% of clips count as two-handed (both hands in at least half the hand frames; ASL Citizen: 55%), so the one-handed rule for the dominant hand doesn't apply; and a model could tell the datasets apart by the resting hand. A position-based rule (a hand counts as absent while resting low, e.g. its wrist below a threshold relative to the shoulders) would make both datasets alike, and webcam users with visible resting hands too. `right_hand` lies on the image's left as in ASL Citizen.
- Resting-hand threshold, in shoulder widths below the mean shoulder height (x corrected for the aspect ratio): ASL Citizen's frame bottom lies at 0.35 / 0.68 / 1.12 (1st / 50th / 99th percentile of 400 clips), so its detected hand wrists are above 0.88 in 99% and above 1.08 in 99.9% of frames; hands appear and vanish at a median ~0.5. MM-WLAuslan's frame bottom is at ~2.4; its resting wrists at the first and last frame lie at 1.25 / 1.54 (5th / 50th percentile), and 41% of its detected hand frames are below 1.0. With the threshold 1.0, the 30-sign sample goes from 2% to 25% one-handed clips and from a median 78 to 57 prepared frames (ASL Citizen: 41–42). Both hands also rest low in two-handed signs, so they are trimmed too.
- Re-preparing ASL Citizen with the threshold: 48,557 clips (2 more excluded), 46,609 identical; 1,209 are trimmed differently and 5 change their dominant hand (hands near the bottom edge of low framings). The GRU retrained on it (`gru_hide_low`, 5 reference draws) gives val EER 0.0586 / 0.0302 and top-1 76.2% / 86.5% at k = 1 / 5, within noise of `gru_reg_aug` (0.0575 / 0.0311, 75.9% / 86.1%): the rule costs ASL Citizen nothing. `gru_hide_low` is the ASL-only baseline for the MM-WLAuslan comparison.
- Fingerspelling in MM-WLAuslan: 24 single-letter glosses (the two-handed Auslan alphabet, A–Z without O and Q; 480 clips), a few initialisms (SMS, UFO, TTY, WFD), and fingerspelled words among the ordinary glosses, unmarked in the labels: the dataset's "Auslan Fingerspelling Gloss" page shows TV, NEWS and TOY, and the sample's TOY clips are indeed fingerspelled (non-dominant hand up with spread fingers, dominant index touching its fingers). - Counting fingerspelled words from the landmarks doesn't work. Matching each frame's two-hand configuration (both hands relative to the non-dominant wrist, dominant index near the non-dominant hand) against frames of the same letter in other clips matches few frames even for the letter clips themselves (none in the median letter clip): MediaPipe often loses a hand while the hands touch, so the fingerspelling configuration is where the landmarks are worst. The known fingerspelled words don't stand out (TOY ranks 78th, NEWS 136th, TELEVISION 164th); the top signs are initialized signs (DAUGHTER, FATHER, FRIDAY, KILOGRAM). - Lookalike check on the 30-sign sample (`gru_hide_low`): same held-out ASL sign by two signer halves 0.855 / 0.942 / 0.981 (5th / 50th / 95th percentile), different signs −0.27 / 0.01 / 0.34 (99.9th 0.68). One of 27 Auslan training signs is in the same-sign range: HOMOSEXUAL next to ASL MAPLE (val/test sign), 0.879. In the videos they are the same sign in form: one hand raised beside the head at shoulder height, palm forward, spread fingers closing so the thumb touches the middle finger (open-8 to 8); only the small movement might differ. Their English labels have nothing in common, so label matching can't find such lookalikes. Conversely, the sample's 3 label-matched pairs (e.g. ELIMINATE / ERASE1, via keywords) are dissimilar (0.04–0.17): label matching is both loose and incomplete.
- Sign overlap with ASL Citizen: Auslan belongs to the BANZSL family (with BSL and NZSL), unrelated to ASL, but some signs look alike (iconic signs, loans); a lexical comparison of basic vocabulary (McKee & Kennedy) found 26–32% of ASL signs identical or similar to Auslan, BSL and NZSL signs. By English label (ignoring case and sense numbers), 130 of 290 ASL Citizen val signs and 110 of 269 test signs match an Auslan gloss (216 and 187 including the Auslan keywords). Conversely, 263 Auslan glosses match an ASL val/test sign by gloss, 597 by gloss or keyword. A matching label does not mean the same sign, and a lookalike sign can have a different label, so labels only locate likely overlaps. An Auslan lookalike of a val/test sign in training would make that sign no longer unseen and inflate the evaluation.
- Lookalike check on the full data (`gru_hide_low`; sign embedding = normalized mean of its 20 clips): 90 of the 2,618 Auslan training signs (3.4%) have a held-out ASL sign at or above the same-sign 5th percentile (0.855), 26 (1.0%) above 0.9; of the 597 label-matched signs left out, 49 (8.2%). So label-matched signs are likely lookalikes 2.4x as often as the others, but label matching misses most of them, and most label matches aren't similar (median 0.21). Among the top pairs are iconic lookalikes (COLD (ILLNESS) / TISSUE, HAIRBRUSH / COMB1, ANTLERS / MOOSE, THANK YOU / THANKYOU, FEAR / COMPLAIN) and hub effects (SOON2 is nearest to SNACK FOOD, DUMMY and POO; PENGUIN and BABY1 are the nearest held-out sign of ~30 Auslan signs each). 38 of 290 val signs and 34 of 269 test signs have an Auslan training sign above 0.855.
- For comparison, 40 of the 559 held-out ASL signs (7.2%) have another held-out ASL sign above 0.855: synonyms and variants that ASL Citizen labels as different signs (MOVIE1 / MOVIE2 0.98, CREATE / MAKE, TRUTH / HONEST, TO / UNTIL, PRICE / COST, GUN1 / GUN2). Same-form signs under different labels are thus more common within ASL Citizen's own vocabulary than Auslan adds them, and they count as negatives in the evaluation (making it somewhat pessimistic).
- ASL Citizen + MM-WLAuslan (`gru_auslan`, 92k training clips of 4,790 signs, 30 epochs of ~17 s, best epoch 16) vs ASL only (`gru_hide_low`), val EER / top-1: 0.0559 / 77.3% vs 0.0586 / 76.2% at k = 1, 0.0294 / 87.7% vs 0.0302 / 86.5% at k = 5. A real but small gain: by the vocabulary scaling (+6 points per doubling of ASL signs), 2.2x the signs would give ~+7 points if Auslan signs were worth ASL signs; the gain corresponds to roughly a tenth of that. Training steps also doubled (more clips per epoch), but longer training alone didn't help earlier.
- No sign that Auslan lookalikes inflate the evaluation: the per-sign top-1 gain at k = 1 is +1.5 points (CI −0.2 to +3.2) for the 38 val signs with an Auslan training sign above 0.855, +0.2 for the 44 at 0.8–0.855, +1.2 for the other 208; correlation of the nearest Auslan similarity with the gain −0.02. Val signs with an Auslan label match (their Auslan matches left out) gain as much as those without (+1.1 vs +1.0). An extra embedding-based exclusion is therefore not needed; the label rule is kept as a cheap precaution (dropping it would add 548 mostly unrelated signs, worth maybe +0.2 points by the above).
- Test split (269 signs by 11 unseen signers, 3,218 clips), `gru_hide_low` vs `gru_auslan`, EER / top-1: 0.0490 / 73.2% vs 0.0460 / 74.8% at k = 1, 0.0263 / 83.8% vs 0.0244 / 85.5% at k = 3, 0.0229 / 86.6% vs 0.0223 / 87.7% at k = 5 (CIs about ±0.005 EER, ±2 points top-1); AUC 0.989 / 0.996 at k = 1 / 5 for both. Again no sign of lookalike inflation: +2.1 points at k = 1 for the 34 test signs with an Auslan training sign above 0.855, +1.7 for the 192 below 0.8 (correlation −0.05). Compared with val, test has a lower EER but lower top-1 at k = 1; the sign sets differ, so absolute val and test numbers aren't directly comparable.
- Operating points of `gru_auslan` on test (one global threshold, 5 reference draws; "similar sign" = the target's most similar other sign among the 269 test signs, by mean embedding). k = 1: at the equal error threshold 4.6% of correct attempts are rejected and 4.6% of attempts of a random other sign accepted, but 74% of attempts of the similar sign; rejecting 5% of correct attempts accepts 4.1% random / 72% similar, rejecting 10% accepts 1.4% / 52%. k = 5: equal error 2.2% (similar sign 75%); rejecting 5% of correct attempts accepts 0.8% random / 51% similar. False rejects are uneven across signs: at the k = 1 equal error threshold, 6.7% of signs reject more than 20% of their correct attempts. The model thus rejects completely wrong signs well but mostly accepts the closest confusable sign; with a larger vocabulary the closest signs are even closer.

- Crawling it (measured 2026-09-18 on ids 1–200, `scripts/download_sts_lexikon.py`): 6 threads fetch ~15 entry pages/s and the videos come down far faster, so the full crawl is well under an hour of page fetching plus the download. Viewing a sample of groups is still needed, but a group with two transcriptions is a class that holds more than one sign.

The vocabulary is 2.5 times Slovo's, on the target language, at no access cost.
- The transcription check holds over the whole crawl: only 16 of the 2,476 classes (0.6%) contain more than one transcription, so the lexicon's same-form links are consistent with its own form notation. Those 16 are the first candidates for viewing, and for exclusion if they turn out to hold two signs.

- Extraction and preparation of the classes (2026-09-18): 8,138 clips in 1 h 44 min (1.3 clips/s) into a 4.9 GB store, prepared to 8,135 clips of 2,476 signs (0.24 GB) with 3 broken clips excluded, which leaves one class (`sts:jacka-00544`) with a single clip. Median 66 frames at 30 fps (2.2 s), 95th percentile 91, and only 0.3% of clips are mirrored, so the studio models sign right-dominant almost without exception — unlike the webcam datasets, this set will barely exercise the mirroring path.
- The clips that take longest are compound words (`teckenspråkskurs`, `deaflympics`, place names such as `Hässelby gård`), median 3.4–4.1 s against 2.2 s overall, so a compound signed as two signs is the shape to check for first when the classes are viewed.
- Six classes viewed as video (2026-09-18, `outputs/clips/sheet_*.jpg` and the matching `sts_*.gif` from `scripts/view_clips.py`): `förälskad-01854` (4 clips), the two worst mixed-transcription classes `skyldig-01412` and `genuin-03881` (9 clips each), the largest class `dynamisk-02104` (18), the compound `teckenspråkskurs-24396` and a two-clip control `musik-01646`. Every class is one sign form, including both mixed-transcription classes, so a second transcription in a group is a difference of detail rather than a second sign, and the largest groups are coherent too. Compounds are real (`teckenspråkskurs` is signed in two parts) but they are one lexical entry, so they stay.
- The same-signer risk is visible in that sample: both clips of `teckenspråkskurs-24396` are by one model in one session, while `förälskad-01854` and `dynamisk-02104` each span three models. Nothing in the metadata distinguishes the two cases, which is exactly what the pseudo signer ids have to supply.
- **The lexicon's resting pose lands exactly on the `max_hand_y` cutoff.** The models rest with their hands clasped in front of the body, in frame and detected, at a median 1.02 shoulder widths below the shoulders (10th–90th percentile 0.91–1.22) — against a cutoff of 1.0. So `hide_low_hands` fires for 56% of clips and not for the other 44%, and in 64 of 120 sampled classes both kinds of clip sit in the same class, with a median within-class spread of 20 percentage points of kept duration. The comparison datasets are nowhere near the boundary: ASL Citizen's resting hands are outside the frame entirely (never detected), MM-WLAuslan rests at 1.52 and Slovo at 0.64 with pre-trimmed clips.
- Trimming is therefore highly sensitive to that one parameter on this dataset: the median fraction of a clip kept is 0.60 at `max_hand_y` 0.6 (12 of 398 clips then fall below `min_hands`), 0.68 at 0.8, 0.85 at 1.0 and 1.00 at 1.2 and above. Choosing it deliberately for the lexicon, rather than inheriting the webcam default, is part of preparing this evaluation set.
- What is *not* anomalous, measured rather than assumed: the share of near-motionless frames at the ends of a prepared clip is comparable to training data (lead 0.10 against ASL Citizen's 0.16, tail 0.15 against 0.15). The real distribution shift is length — a median 66 prepared frames against ASL Citizen's 42 — which is the careful citation form, not the resting pose.
- The whole lexicon as a glossary (2026-09-18): the 13,554 entries outside a sign class took 3 h 50 min to extract (1.25 clips/s, 11 GB) and were merged with the 8,138 class clips into `data/processed/sts_lexikon/` (21,692 clips, 16 GB; the two parts stay as `sts_lexikon_classes` and `sts_lexikon_rest` until deleted). Prepared as `data/prepared/sts_lexikon-d314433a/`: 21,689 clips of 16,030 signs (2,475 with several clips, 13,555 with one; the same 3 broken clips excluded), 0.71 GB, median 71 frames. The entries outside the classes run a little longer, and the longest (compound words and place names such as `speciallärarutbildning`, `Östra centrum`) reach the 128-frame cap, so they are compressed in time.
- Evaluating against that glossary works at full scale once only the query rows are scored (`draw_scores` with `rows`): 50 queries against 21,639 clips of 16,030 signs take 38 s and 4.4 GB, where scoring every clip would have taken ~14 GB. It also exposed a crash that any recordings-against-lexicon evaluation would have hit: `np.unique` cannot sort the lexicon's null signers among the recordings' ids (fixed by `evaluation.signer_codes`).
- A first, optimistic look at the lexicon as a glossary: one clip of each of 50 random sign classes as the query, scored against the rest of the lexicon (so against the class's other recordings, among all 16,030 signs), with `gru_auslan_phonpool1` at k = 1: EER 0.033 [0.012, 0.060], AUC 0.995, top-1 52% [39, 65], top-5 72%. Studio clips against studio clips, possibly by the same model signer, and few queries, so this bounds nothing about recordings; but it suggests the pattern to expect: verification against one sign holds up, while picking the right sign out of 16,000 is much harder than out of ASL Citizen's 269 test signs (top-1 76%).
- **No height cutoff separates rest from signing in the lexicon**, so lowering `max_hand_y` is not the fix. Its resting frames sit at p10–p90 of 0.89–1.11, but its signing frames reach the same depth (p90 = 1.02): a cutoff of 0.8 hides 96.9% of resting frames and 29.3% of signing frames with them, 1.0 hides 59.4% and 12.2%, and 1.2 hides 1.3% and 0.3%. Frames hidden inside the sign become gaps that only `max_gap` interpolates over. If the rest pose should go, the discriminator has to be stillness at the clip ends, not height; if it should stay, a cutoff of 1.2 keeps it consistently at almost no cost to the sign.
- A global cutoff of 0.8 would also be badly aimed: ASL Citizen's frame bottom is at 0.69, so its resting hands are already outside the frame and 0.8 costs it only 1.1% of signing frames (0.1% at 1.0) — a near no-op where the rule came from — while WLASL (13% of clips), MM-WLAuslan (15%) and Slovo (9%) lose sign content they keep today, and MM-WLAuslan already loses 19.9% of its signing frames at 1.0. Any change is also expensive to adopt: `PrepConfig.id()` hashes the whole config, so a new value re-prepares every dataset and makes the recorded model numbers incomparable until `gru_auslan_phonpool1` is retrained.

MediaPipe extraction speed (machine: Ryzen 9 5950X, 16 cores / 32 threads; RTX 3090):
- HolisticLandmarker on the CPU, one process: ~22 ms per frame wall, ~40 ms CPU (uses ~1.7 cores).
- Parallel throughput peaks at 8 worker processes (~136 frames/s in short benchmarks, ~150 expected with long-lived workers) and drops with more workers (16: ~103, 32: ~84). CPU time per frame doubles at 8 workers (contention for physical cores, SMT, clock speed). Pinning workers to cores makes it slower (8 pinned: 107). The Python API does not expose MediaPipe's thread count.
- GPU: the HolisticLandmarker fails on the GPU (its face blendshapes model has unsupported operations, and the bundle cannot be built without it). The separate FaceLandmarker, HandLandmarker and PoseLandmarker run on the GPU (~6 ms per frame each, ~18 ms together), but multi-process throughput saturates at ~64 frames/s: MediaPipe's OpenGL path processes one frame at a time with CPU-GPU synchronization, and model loading takes 0.5–2 s per video. Using the GPU well would require reimplementing MediaPipe's pipeline with batched inference.

Browser extraction (2026-09-19, `scripts/compare_browser_extraction.py`: the 54 Swedish recordings, 4,520 frames, extracted in headless Chrome 151 with `@mediapipe/tasks-vision` 1.0.1 on the CPU and compared with the Python store):
- Nothing blocks it. The web library has the same `HolisticLandmarker` at the same version as the Python package and loads the same `.task` file (13.7 MB, plus about 12 MB of WebAssembly). Playwright's own Chromium can't decode H.264, so the test uses the installed Google Chrome (`channel="chrome"`).
- The landmarks are close but not identical (the browser decodes and converts the video itself, and its WebAssembly inference differs numerically). Face: median distance 0.0004 shoulder widths, detection differs in 1 frame. Pose: detected in every frame by both, median 0.014, 95th percentile 0.15 (the largest distances are the out-of-frame lower-body points, which preparation drops). Hands: median 0.006 (left) and 0.003 (right), 95th percentile 0.05–0.09, but detection differs in 17% (left) and 9% (right) of the frames where either extraction found the hand, mostly borderline frames at the start and end of the sign. The hand detections at the edges move the trim: 21 of 51 prepared clips have the same length, most others differ by 1–2 frames, the most by 7.

For comparison, two takes of the same sign by the same signer have a median of 0.914, and different signs 0.115.
